\section{Extensions}

The same pattern holds across all extensions: messages that rarely and predictably change the receiver's answer are certified (the Llama-8B receiver, Text+fact nominally, SQuAD, and sampled answers, on OBQA nominally). A stronger helper for the medium receiver falls back because its calibration splits are too small, while the remaining settings fall back (the OLMo and Llama pairs, GSM8K). Each ran under a protocol fixed before its outputs (Appendix~\ref{app:medium}). Two prospective tests of the label-light certificate, on a three-agent system and on a 32B pair, close this appendix (Appendix~\ref{app:prospective}).

\subsection{Cross-family receivers and helpers}\label{app:xfam}

Each cross-family pair ran the main study's protocol, using prompts in each model's native chat template, under its own frozen protocol, with every setting reported. The OLMo pair combines the large helper (whose saved messages serve as Text messages, matching token counts on every row) with an OLMo-2-1124-7B-Instruct receiver \citep{olmo2025olmo2}, for which no C2C fuser exists (a 60-test family). The Llama pair combines a Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct helper \citep{grattafiori2024llama3} with the small receiver on OBQA and ARC (an 80-test family). Its C2C outputs come from the official evaluator and cross-family fuser run on our rows, with receiver prompts byte-identical to ours across all 5,626 rows and 16 saved small-pair C2C outputs reproduced bit for bit. All seven settings fall back (Table~\ref{tab:dev_full}). OLMo disagrees with Text on 23.2, 22.4, and 42.1\% of OBQA, ARC, and MMLU-Pro development questions (Qwen3-8B: 9.7, 5.0, and 21.2\%), at AUROC 0.790, 0.749, and 0.633 (0.873, 0.952, and 0.821), and answers 71.6, 68.2, and 22.4\% correctly alone. The Llama helper raises the small receiver's accuracy from 38.4 to 44.9 (Text) and 48.2\% (C2C) on OBQA, and from 36.8 to 48.5 and 48.8\% on ARC. Its Text messages change fewer answers than the Qwen helper's (25.7 and 29.4\% against 45.7 and 48.5\%), but they are ranked weakly (AUROC 0.693 and 0.678), and the fuser changes 57.5 and 58.2\%.

Part of the OLMo fallback stems from answer format, as unparseable outputs count as changes: 4.3, 9.2, and 17.1\% of OLMo's receiver-only and 5.9, 8.0, and 13.1\% of its Text calibration answers are \textsc{invalid} (on development 2.8, 12.0, and 17.0\% and 3.5, 6.4, and 13.7\%; Llama-pair receiver 3.4 and 10.7\%, references 2.7 to 6.9\%; Llama-8B pair at most 0.1\%). Furthermore, 72 of 320, 47 of 95, and 968 of 2,457 calibration disagreements involve exactly one \textsc{invalid} output. A non-deployable, post-hoc filter retaining only questions where both answers parse certifies all three OLMo settings ($q=0.45$, 0.40, and 0.05; development disagreement 18.8, 15.5, and 33.5\%, AUROC 0.855, 0.881, and 0.701) but no Llama setting. No deployable rule we tested certifies OLMo or any Llama setting. Post hoc, answering skipped questions with the probe's argmax agrees with OLMo's own answer on 95.2, 90.2, and 81.1\% of calibration questions but passes no threshold (smallest $p=0.901$, 0.377, and 1.000; AUROC 0.808, 0.765, and 0.653), because the reference's unparseable answers (15.8, 29.2, and 31.2\% of development disagreements) remain changes; routing unparseable receiver answers to the reference also fails (Appendix~\ref{app:restson}).

\paragraph{Llama-8B pair.} A Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct receiver reading the large helper's saved messages was added after these fell back, as the first of three candidates in an order fixed before any output (followed by Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 and Granite-3.1-8B-Instruct, which produced no outputs, making OLMo-2 the only other non-Qwen receiver run). It used its default chat template and a 40-test family. It disagrees with Text on 11.6 and 12.4\% of OBQA and ARC development questions (AUROC 0.874 and 0.880) and is certified at $q=0.60$ (21 of 800 skipped calibration answers change, $p=5.7\times10^{-4}$) and $q=0.70$ (4 of 320, $p=3.2\times10^{-4}$). On development, 7 of 438 (1.60\% [0.64, 3.27]) and 4 of 206 (1.94\% [0.53, 4.90]) skipped answers change. Both deploy in all 200 re-drawn splits and keep their quantile on questions whose two answers parse and with argmax answers; no score is exactly zero. Text raises accuracy from 603 to 636 of 742 and from 239 to 260 of 299, with the policies answering 638 and 258 correctly.

\subsection{Informed and stronger helpers}\label{app:informed}

\paragraph{Text+fact.} Post hoc, the large helper's input gains one line before the question, \texttt{Useful fact: <fact1>}, providing the OpenBookQA science fact behind the question. Consequently, the receiver sees the fact only through the message. Receiver-only outputs, scores, and thresholds are reused in a separate 20-test family. The message changes 12.4\% of calibration answers (170 of 1,366; plain Text 10.5\%) and 10.2\% of development answers (AUROC 0.889). The test deploys $q=0.75$ (22 of 999, $p=4.8\times10^{-6}$; $q=0.80$ fails, 33 of 1,064, $p=0.0016$), also without the 208 reviewed questions (20 of 852). The certificate is nominal because ProbeMax was chosen on the plain Text labels of the same questions; held out, 16 of 558 skipped answers change ($p=9.2\times10^{-3}$). On development, the policy skips 536 of 742 questions and changes 13. The message corrects 62 development answers and breaks 9; the policy skips 11 of the corrections and 1 of the breaks, answering 660 of 742 correctly against 670 for Text+fact and 617 for $R$ ($-1.35$ points [$-2.29$, $-0.54$], within $\kappa\alpha\approx3.6$). This is the only deployed policy whose development accuracy interval lies below zero.

\paragraph{A stronger helper.} Under a protocol fixed before its outputs, the large helper's stored messages were given to the medium receiver on OBQA and ARC (40 tests). The helper alone answers 86.3 and 87.6\% of development questions correctly (receiver: 66.0 and 72.6\%). Its messages raise the receiver's accuracy by 7.3 and 4.3 points but change 21.2 and 13.4\% of its development answers (calibration: 297 of 1,366 and 55 of 448; AUROC 0.792 and 0.800). Both settings fall back (smallest $p=0.26$ and 0.026); they certify in none and 15.5\% of 200 re-drawn splits, and would need about 21,500 and 880 calibration questions to pass.

\paragraph{Design and selection.} A pilot protocol fixed before any pilot output defined eight candidates (SQuAD and NQ with passages, TriviaQA and NQ-Open closed-book \citep{rajpurkar2016squad,kwiatkowski2019nq,fisch2019mrqa,joshi2017triviaqa,lee2019latent}, each with a Qwen3-8B and a Llama-8B receiver and the large helper), three scores, and a label-free selection rule. A full-run protocol fixed before any full-run output added a 1,000-question test split and narrowed the reporting rule, so that an unqualified claim for extractive questions required a passing test as well as a certificate (recorded deviations from the pilot protocol); the certificate was fixed before any development or test output existed. For SQuAD, the training pool is deduplicated and limited to passages of at most 300 words (86,830 questions), and all splits are disjoint blocks of a fixed pseudorandom order. The receiver answers directly with at most 32 new tokens, and answers are compared using official SQuAD normalization. The three scores are one minus the top probability at the first generated token containing a letter or digit (analogous to ProbeMax), one minus the whole answer's probability, and one minus its per-token geometric mean; the whole-answer score had the best pilot AUROC in all eight settings. It needs the receiver-only answer, which is served when the message is skipped and wasted otherwise. A setting was eligible if at most 4\% of its 25\% lowest-score pilot questions changed; eligible settings were ranked by the certification probability $P$ that the frozen binormal model (Appendix~\ref{app:predict}) predicts from the pilot's disagreement rate and AUROC, and at most two with $P\ge0.5$ would run, each at the smallest calibration size with $P\ge0.8$. The four Qwen3-8B settings and NQ-Open with the Llama-8B receiver are ineligible (7 to 30 of 100 changed), and the rule selected SQuAD with the Llama-8B receiver at 2,000 calibration questions ($P=0.975$, inflated by taking the best of eight; conservative 0.081) and no second setting (next: 0.413 and 0.393; Table~\ref{tab:squad_pilot}).

\paragraph{Results.} Before production, the runtime reproduced 16 pilot questions exactly. On calibration, the receiver disagrees with Text on 302 of 2,000 questions (15.1\%), and the test deploys $q=0.35$ (17 of 721, $p=2.5\times10^{-4}$; Table~\ref{tab:squad_cal}). All 200 re-drawn splits certify (median $q=0.375$, median development coverage 41.5\%). On development (disagreement 15.4\% [13.2, 17.8], AUROC 0.811 [0.771, 0.848]), the policy skips 39.6\% of questions, and 16 of 396 skipped answers change (4.04\% [2.3, 6.5]). In 15 of these, one normalized answer contains the other; counting containment as agreement leaves 1. On the sealed test, 9 of 364 skipped answers change (2.47\% [1.14, 4.64], $p=0.012$); the pass rule is a two-sided 95\% upper bound below 5\%, so the test passes, though $p$ exceeds 0.001. On development and test, $R$ answers 659 and 664 of 1,000 questions correctly, Text 669 and 682, and the policy 673 and 686. Among the skipped changes, Text would have corrected 4 and 2 answers and broken 8 and 6. Text's development gain, 1.0 point [$-0.9$, $+2.9$], is below $\kappa\alpha=2.0$, and its interval includes zero, so the protocol's redundancy rule holds. From recorded per-question times (sequential execution, on a different machine than the \S\ref{sec:rq3} replays), skipping saves an estimated 198.5~ms [167.2, 229.9] per question, while always skipping saves 785.4~ms (helper 772.5~ms; receiver 153.5~ms alone and 166.4~ms with the message, since the receiver-only answer is always computed). This is an estimate, not a replay.

\subsection{A numeric-answer check: GSM8K}\label{app:gsm}

A prespecified check (large pair, Text) used 400 fit, 1,000 calibration, and 400 development training questions in a fixed content-based order (a recorded deviation from the main protocol's seeded shuffle) and raised the receiver's limit from 64 to 320 tokens (a deviation fixed in advance), because the receiver derives before answering. A change is a mismatch of the normalized final number (after the last ``answer is'', otherwise the last number; the extractor was tested on twelve hand-written strings and, as prespecified, never changed). The score is one minus the length-normalized probability of the receiver's answer tokens under teacher forcing, which reduces to ProbeMax for a single label token. No candidate passes (every $p=1.000$), although all 3,200 outputs parse and the receiver disagrees with Text on 17.4\% of calibration and 19.3\% of development questions (accuracy 87.75\% alone, 86.50\% with Text). The score fails: 378 of 400 fit questions score exactly zero after the derivation, so 18 of the 20 thresholds coincide. Its development AUROC is 0.5795 (0.6444 for the well-spread whole-continuation probability, 0.5680 for the first answer token). The last-number rule fired on 15.2\% of receiver-only outputs, tracking the 320-token cap (reached by 43 fit, 106 calibration, and 49 development outputs); the Text path is barely affected (6 of 1,000 and 2 of 400). A later protocol, written after this fallback, planned a score over the first 32 generated tokens but stopped at its reproduction check (differences up to 0.04 and 0.06 on two capped outputs, against a tolerance of $10^{-4}$), so no prefix score was tested (Table~\ref{tab:provenance}).

\subsection{Sampled receiver answers}\label{app:sampling}

Post hoc, under a protocol fixed before its outputs, the large pair's OBQA and ARC Text settings were rerun with receiver answers sampled on both paths (the Qwen3 model card's settings with thinking disabled: temperature 0.7, top-$p$ 0.8, top-$k$ 20, min-$p$ 0; one sample per question and path, seeded independently), with messages, scores, thresholds, and the test unchanged in a 40-test family (OBQA stays nominal). Both settings deploy at the greedy thresholds ($q=0.80$: 19 of 1,064, $p=3.4\times10^{-8}$; $q=0.95$: 4 of 422, $p=4.8\times10^{-6}$) and in all 200 re-drawn splits, and 3.32\% [2.01, 5.14] and 2.82\% [1.22, 5.47] of skipped development answers change. Sampled disagreement is 9.4 and 4.7\% (AUROC 0.867 and 0.956; greedy: 9.7 and 5.0\%), most skipped questions still have zero uncertainty (354 of 572 and 217 of 284), and accuracy is 617 and 646 of 742 (receiver alone, Text) and 268 and 275 of 299. Sampling changes few of this receiver's labels (6 of 742 receiver-only and 2 Text answers on OBQA, 0 and 1 of 299 on ARC), so this check says nothing about receivers whose samples vary more or that generate reasoning tokens.

\subsection{Prospective tests: a three-agent system and a 32B pair}\label{app:prospective}\label{app:e1}\label{app:e2b}

The label-light certificate of Appendix~\ref{app:accuracy} was analyzed post hoc. Two tests apply it prospectively: one on a three-agent system and a dataset used nowhere else in this work, and one on the Text path with both agents scaled to 32B parameters (OBQA is used throughout this paper, so the second test extends model scale, not data). For each test, the protocol (models, prompts, score, candidate thresholds, tests, label rule, policies, and pass rules) was committed before any calibration, development, or unseen-question output of its models existed (for the three-agent system, committed and time-stamped), and the unseen questions were evaluated once. Both primary policies meet every pass rule (Table~\ref{tab:prospective}).

\paragraph{Three-agent system.} Agents A and B are Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and the receiver C is Qwen3-8B with thinking disabled. A (background) reads the question and choices under the helper instruction of Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} and writes one background message (at most 256 new tokens). B (verifier) reads the question and choices, the two QASC facts, and A's message, and writes a checked and extended background (256). C answers (64) from A's turn on the cheap path, or from A's and B's turns on the reference path. The reference $b$ runs all three agents; the cheap path, which takes the place of $R$, omits B. One decision is made per question: after A's message, the probe on C's cheap-path input decides whether C waits for B. C sees only the agents' turns, in the receiver template of Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}: A's turn (helper instruction, then A's message) and, on the reference path, B's turn, whose user content is B's instruction without the two facts, A's note, and the clauses that introduce them, followed by B's message. As for the Text+fact receiver (Appendix~\ref{app:informed}), the facts reach C only through B's message. B's user message is the first line below, the question block of Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}, the two facts, A's note, and the closing rule; in C's view of B's turn, the two bracketed clauses, the facts, and the note are absent.
{\fontsize{6.3}{7.0}\selectfont
\begin{verbatim}
In at most three clear sentences, state the background knowledge needed to answer the question below.
[ Another assistant has already written a background note for this question: check that note, correct
anything in it that is wrong, and add the knowledge it is missing.] [ Use the two reference facts given
below as your source.]

{question}

Reference facts:
1. {fact1}
2. {fact2}

Background note from another assistant:
{A's message}

Do NOT directly solve or give answer to the question: do not say which choice is correct, do not discuss
or rule out any of the choices, and do NOT mention any option letter.
\end{verbatim}}
\noindent Each question's A and B messages are generated once and shared by both paths and the probe. All models run in bfloat16 with SDPA attention, greedy decoding, and batch size one; the parser (version 2) is extended to labels A to H. QASC's training and development splits contain 9,060 eight-option questions, each with two supporting facts; the official test split has neither answers nor facts and is not used. Deduplicating by question id and then by normalized question and choices leaves 9,051 questions. A fixed-seed shuffle assigns 2,000 to fit, 3,000 to calibration, 1,000 to development, and 2,000 to the sealed test; the remaining 1,051 were never processed by any model. QASC is public, so the split is independent of our design process, not of the models' pretraining data.

\paragraph{32B pair.} The helper agent is Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and the receiver agent is Qwen3-32B with thinking disabled. The reference is the Text path and the cheap path is the receiver agent alone, with the prompts, decoding, and parser of the main experiments (Appendices~\ref{app:protocol} and~\ref{app:prompts}). Both models run in bfloat16 in Hugging Face Transformers with greedy decoding and batch size one, each across two A100-SXM4-40GB GPUs of one node, and the same backend is used from fit to evaluation. The splits are those of the main OBQA experiments: 2,100 fit, 1,366 calibration, 742 development, and 744 held-out questions. A pilot on the first 300 fit questions was to decide only whether gain retention would be emphasized (at least 6 more correct answers with Text): Text answered 276 and the receiver agent alone 271. The full protocol was committed to run in either case before the pilot result.

\paragraph{Score.} Each label's probability sums the next-token probabilities over its token set and is renormalized over the labels (A to H on QASC, A to D on OBQA). The score is the log of the non-top mass, $u=\log\sum_{l\ne l^\ast}p_l$, computed as a log-sum-exp in FP32; it orders questions as $1-\max_l p_l$ does wherever that is nonzero. On the QASC fit split, $1-\max_l p_l$ is exactly zero in FP32 for 1,143 of 2,000 questions (57.15\%), which would merge the first 11 candidate thresholds, whereas $u$ takes 1,928 distinct values. This is the tie-breaking score of Appendix~\ref{app:restson}, chosen from the fit-split score distribution before any calibration output existed. A non-finite score routes to the reference (none occurred in either test).

\paragraph{Tests, labels, and pass rules.} The 20 candidates and the change-rate test are those of Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}, applied to $u$ (a candidate that skips no question fails), and the lost-correction test is that of Appendix~\ref{app:accuracy}, with all $N$ calibration questions as its denominator (notation and $p$-values: Table~\ref{tab:prospective_cal}). Gold labels are queried in increasing $\tau$, only for newly skipped disagreements; a candidate is decided after all its disagreements are labeled; for each $\varepsilon$ the scan stops at the first candidate that fails the lost-correction test; $\varepsilon=0.02$ runs first, and no label is queried beyond the largest candidate that passes the change-rate test. The label-free policy is the largest candidate passing the change-rate test, and the joint policies are the largest passing both tests at $\varepsilon=0.01$ (primary) and at $\varepsilon=0.02$. With 20 change-rate and 40 lost-correction tests at level 0.001, all calibration conclusions of a test hold jointly with probability at least 0.94 (Bonferroni). The pass rules on the unseen questions use the upper end of the two-sided 95\% Clopper--Pearson interval, as in the main text: R1, changes among skipped questions below 5\%; R2, lost corrections over all unseen questions below $\varepsilon$ (on 744 questions, at most 2 lost corrections at $\varepsilon=0.01$ and 7 at $\varepsilon=0.02$); the lower ends of the paired-bootstrap 95\% intervals (2,000 resamples) of the reference's gain $G$ and the policy's gain $G_\pi$, both over the cheap path, above zero; and a saving on the development timing panel whose lower end is above zero. ``Keeps at least half of the gain'' requires the lower end of $G_\pi-G/2$ to exceed zero.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Table~\ref{tab:prospective_cal} lists every candidate. For the three-agent system, the label-free policy is $j=11$ (calibration coverage 53.7\%), the primary policy is $j=8$ (39.0\%), and the $\varepsilon=0.02$ policy is $j=10$ (48.2\%). The label rule queried 40 of the 3,000 calibration labels, all disagreements at $j\le11$: 39 were lost corrections and one was wrong on both paths. Before calibration, the label-free plug-in rule of Appendix~\ref{app:predict}, applied to fit counts, predicted the label-free policy at $j=12$; $j=12$ narrowly fails ($p=0.0012$). For the 32B pair, the label-free policy is $j=17$ (83.7\%), the primary policy is $j=14$ (67.6\%), and the $\varepsilon=0.02$ policy is $j=16$ (79.0\%). The label rule queried 21 of the 1,366 calibration labels, all disagreements at $j\le17$: 13 were lost corrections, 7 were questions the receiver agent alone answers correctly and Text does not, and one was wrong on both paths. On fit, Text answers 92.95\% and the receiver agent alone 90.29\%.

\paragraph{Unseen questions.} Table~\ref{tab:prospective} gives every policy and both reference gains. On the 2,000 sealed QASC questions, all three policies pass R1, both joint policies pass R2 at their $\varepsilon$, and all three keep more than half of the gain: $G_\pi-G/2$ is 6.15 [5.40, 6.95], 5.70 [4.97, 6.50], and 5.15 [4.40, 5.93] points for the primary, $\varepsilon=0.02$, and label-free policies. B changes 16.4\% of C's answers (328 of 2,000), yet only 5 of these changes fall among the primary policy's 798 skipped questions. On the 744 held-out OBQA questions, Text changes the 32B receiver agent's answer on 45 questions (6.0\%). All three policies pass R1, both joint policies pass R2 at their $\varepsilon$, and all three keep a gain whose interval excludes zero. The primary policy's two changed answers are one lost correction and one question that only the receiver agent alone answers correctly, so its accuracy equals that of the reference. The joint policies keep at least half of the gain ($G_\pi-G/2$ of 1.28 [0.40, 2.15] and 1.01 [0.13, 1.88]); for the label-free policy the lower end is zero (0.87 [0.00, 1.75]), so we do not make that statement, and it does not pass R2 at $\varepsilon=0.01$.

\paragraph{Development questions and single models (32B pair).} The development questions decide nothing and are reported descriptively. The primary, $\varepsilon=0.02$, and label-free policies skip 69.3, 80.6, and 85.4\% of the 742 questions, change 1 of 514, 5 of 598, and 10 of 634 skipped answers (upper bounds 1.08, 1.94, and 2.88\%), and lose 1, 2, and 6 corrections (upper bounds 0.75, 0.97, and 1.75\%). Text's gain is smaller there, $G=1.08$ points [$-0.54$, 2.70] (91.91\% for the receiver agent alone against 92.99\%), and neither it nor the policies' gains exclude zero: 0.94 [$-0.67$, 2.43], 1.21 [$-0.27$, 2.70], and 0.81 [$-0.54$, 2.16] points, at accuracies of 92.86, 93.13, and 92.72\%. The helper agent answering alone, with the same free-generation protocol as the other paths, reaches 91.26\% on the held-out questions and 91.91\% on development; the pair with Text is 1.61 points [$-0.27$, 3.63] above it on the held-out questions.

\paragraph{Latency.} Latency is measured live at batch size one on 128-question development panels, on exclusively allocated nodes, and every policy saves time (Table~\ref{tab:prospective_latency}). For the three-agent system the panel is the first 128 development questions in a fixed pseudorandom order. A and B generate on one GPU and C and the probe run on another; the policy and the reference alternate per request in counterbalanced order; there is no warm-up, cold requests are kept, and each policy ran once. The replay reproduced the offline records (A's messages and both answers on all 256 requests, B's messages on all 205 requests that ran B, and $u$ on all 128 questions). Skipped queries avoid B (about 1.3~s each), whereas queries that wait for B pay the probe (about 40~ms), so 56\% of the primary policy's panel queries run slightly slower and its paired median saving is $-22$~ms. For the 32B pair the panel is the OBQA development panel of the main experiments. The policy, the Text reference, and the receiver agent alone run in rotating order, in four segments of 32 questions, the last three after untimed warm-up requests on questions outside the panel. The replayed policy answers equal the offline answers on every request, and dropping the first question of each segment changes no conclusion (primary policy: 1,377~ms [1,213, 1,538] on 124 questions). The replay records whole-request latency, not component times, so the table splits the paired difference by route: a skipped question saves about 1.86~s, and a question that waits pays about 90~ms for the probe. The receiver agent alone takes 486~ms.

\paragraph{Controls fixed in the three-agent protocol.} On development (descriptive; Table~\ref{tab:e1_controls}), $G=14.4$ points [12.2, 16.7], and the label-free, primary, and $\varepsilon=0.02$ policies skip 54.9, 39.1, and 48.4\% of questions. C reading the two facts directly is more accurate than the pipeline (+15.7 points [13.3, 18.2]) and faster, so when the facts can be handed to C, the pipeline can be simplified. The experiment tests skipping within a fixed information flow, not the choice of architecture. B without A is also more accurate than A+B+C, and A with a fact-free B matches A+C (63.6 vs.\ 63.1\%). On fit, C alone answers 60.1\% and C reading the facts 92.7\%. A's message names an option in 9.55, 11.1, and 9.85\% of fit, development, and sealed questions, and B's message contains the correct option's text in 72.0, 72.4, and 72.2\%.


\paragraph{Deviations.} Every difference between a protocol and its execution was recorded as it occurred; the full records are released with the dataset. Table~\ref{tab:e1_deviations} lists all thirteen for the three-agent system. For the 32B pair, five entries change no model input, score, test, policy, or pass rule. Before any formal output, two option strings that an anonymization step had altered in our copy of the inputs were restored to the original OBQA text. The label log-probabilities behind the score and the component times of the replay were not saved; the score code, which was fixed before the run, and the route split above stand in for them. The other three concern job scheduling: one-hour shards, each gated by an exact match on two regenerated pilot questions, shard sizes and a submission rule reset from measured times after the pilot, and one resubmission after a refusal for lack of disk space.
